  body: >-
    The house had a galley kitchen five metres long and 2.4 metres wide, with
    a full height wall between it and the living room. Every owner of a house

decision:
  heading: One steel beam at ceiling level, and no bulkhead
  body: >-
    The straightforward answer was a beam under the existing ceiling with a
    bulkhead boxed around it. It is cheaper, it is faster, and it leaves a
    permanent 400mm deep reminder of where the wall used to be, running across
    the middle of the room the entire project was meant to open up. The
    alternative was to lift the beam up into the ceiling space, flush with the
    joists, which meant re-hanging every joist off the new beam with proprietary
    hangers and replacing the ceiling across the full span rather than
    patching. That added roughly $14,000 and eight working days. We showed the
    owners a laser line on the wall at 400mm below the ceiling so they could
    see what the bulkhead would actually feel like at full scale, then gave
    them the two numbers. They took about a minute.

## What the house was

A 1966 brick veneer on a 640 square metre lot in West Ryde, bought by the
current owners in 2019. Three bedrooms, a bathroom that had been redone in the
1990s and did not need touching, and the original kitchen. The house had good
bones and one bad room, which is the ideal brief — all of the budget went into
the part of the house that was actually failing.

The ceilings were the original 2.4 metre height with a dropped section over the
kitchen. The floor was hardwood under three separate layers of covering,
including the vinyl tiles that turned out to contain asbestos. Sanded back, it
came up well enough that replacing it was never seriously discussed.

## On engineered stone

The owners had chosen an engineered stone benchtop from a showroom before they
came to us. Engineered stone can no longer lawfully be supplied or installed in
Australia, so that was not an option, and we would not have offered it in any
case. They went to a porcelain slab with a mitred edge, which cost slightly
more, looks close to what they had originally chosen, and carries none of the
silica risk to the people who fabricate and install it.

## What we would do differently

Open up behind every built-in appliance during the pre-price investigation, not
just the accessible wall cavities. Pulling a wall oven out takes twenty minutes
and would have found that heat shield before the contract was signed rather
than on day three. It is now on our standard pre-price checklist for any house
built before 1990.
